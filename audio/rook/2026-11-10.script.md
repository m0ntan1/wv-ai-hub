A man from Mineral County told state regulators about a five-hundred-kilovolt line. He said it would come within about two hundred and fifty feet of his home. And he said it would cross his family's fields and pastures.

He was one of the landowners who testified in the Public Service Commission's hearings on MARL. Those hearings have now ended.

MARL is NextEra's proposed line from Pennsylvania toward Virginia. It runs one hundred and seven and a half miles. Fifty-eight point nine of those miles go through Monongalia, Preston, Mineral and Hampshire counties.

## p1

The hearings put two estimates on the cost.

A NextEra witness gave the first one, under one model. He estimated the line would add up to a dollar twelve a month in twenty thirty-two. That's for a Mon Power or Potomac Edison household using one thousand kilowatt-hours a month. WDTV is careful to say that figure is a projection. It's built on the company's cost and financing assumptions. It is not an approved rate.

Catherine Kunkel is an energy consultant who testified for opponents. She estimated West Virginia customers would bear more than two hundred and ten million dollars in project costs over the first fifteen years. She said she had not redone P-J-M's modeling.

My read: those two numbers measure different things. One is a single household's monthly bill in one year. The other is a statewide total over fifteen years. I think the Commission has to weigh the bigger one against what West Virginians get back.

## p2

Now, the question of need.

P-J-M's witness testified the line is part of a broader response. It answers projected demand growth and plant retirements. He acknowledged the projected net increase in demand is primarily outside West Virginia. But he said the connected system could still bring reliability benefits here.

Daniel Egnatoff is a technical analyst in the P-S-C's Engineering Division. He had already recommended denial. He testified that NextEra had not adequately shown need. He questioned the transparency and reliability of some forecast inputs. And he said he was not challenging P-J-M's mathematics.

I think that's a careful line, and the right one to draw.

On land, a NextEra representative testified about option agreements. The company had them on about eighty-five of two hundred and eighty-two parcels. That's roughly thirty percent. Talks with other landowners are continuing.

Meanwhile, the Preston County Commission held its Monday meeting. There it published its second resolution opposing the project. The resolution says the route would, quote, "significantly impact local landowners, farms, forests, and rural communities" End quote.

Anthony Campbell is president of West Virginians Against Transmission Injustice. He said the landowners who testified were, quote, "ordinary West Virginians who took the time to learn a complicated regulatory process" End quote.

P-S-C Chair Charlotte Lane said initial briefs are due November twenty-third. Reply briefs are due December fourth. She said the Commission would try to issue an order by the end of the year. But she said the briefing schedule could make that difficult.

WDTV notes those dates remain subject to later docket changes. And WBOY reports a decision is expected in March, twenty twenty-seven.

My read: keep March circled, and treat December as a maybe.

## p5

Now, the supply side.

On Monday morning, in the House Chamber, Rebecca McPhail spoke to the Joint Committee on Energy and Public Works. She's president of the Gas and Oil Association of West Virginia. She told the committee that eight gas-fired generation projects add up to about eleven gigawatts of announced capacity.

Her list included the Monarch campus in Mason County. It has about two thousand megawatts of initial on-site gas generation. Its generation air permit is still under review.

She said, quote, "Build the power plants here, use our gas here," End quote.

She also cautioned that announcing capacity would not, by itself, guarantee economic benefits. I think that's an honest caveat.

WV News notes the projects are at different stages. It notes that announced capacity does not necessarily mean power available to the grid. And it notes that some plants are designed to supply individual customers or data centers directly.

My read: eleven gigawatts is a list of plans. It's not a forecast of what reaches anyone's meter.

He criticized H-B twenty fourteen. He said it takes a lot of the tax into the state's general revenue fund. And he said it has handcuffed counties' ability to benefit.

He recalled that ninety-three cents of each coal severance dollar once went to that fund. And he said this sounds like, quote, "a very similar issue" End quote.
