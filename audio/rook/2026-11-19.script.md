## p0

On Wednesday, State Treasurer Larry Pack stood in front of the historic vault in his Capitol office. He laid out what he wants from the twenty twenty-seven legislative session. That session opens January thirteenth.

Teacher pay made the list. So did term limits. And so did H-B twenty fourteen, the twenty twenty-five data center law. The Dominion Post calls it Governor Patrick Morrisey's signature legislation.

Pack said he was, quote, "amazed that people know a House bill number." End quote.

## p1

His complaint is about money. Or in his words, quote, "the dollar portion of it." End quote.

The Dominion Post reports that Pack wants the law changed. He wants data center property taxes to go back directly to county commissions and county boards of education.

I opened the enrolled bill to see the formula he's fighting. It covers what the bill calls the tax increment. That's the regular-levy taxes on the value a certified High Impact Data Center adds above its base value. The base value is what the assessor had on the books on July first of the year before certification.

Here's how that increment gets split. Fifty percent goes to a Personal Income Tax Reduction Fund. Thirty percent goes to the county where the data center sits. Ten percent goes to all counties, by population. Five percent goes to the Economic Enhancement Grant Fund. And five percent goes to the Electric Grid Stabilization and Security Fund.

The Dominion Post's list leaves out that last five percent.

## p2

Here's the line that stood out to me, by the paper's account. Pack said a county school system with regular levies would get zero property tax dollars from data centers.

Here's how I'd call it. None of the five shares names a school board. But the bill still sends excess levies to the bodies that levy them. Those are figured on the property's full current value.

Regular levies on the base value still go where current law sends them. And bond levies figured on the base value go to the bodies with bonds outstanding.

So my read is this. The zero in the paper's account is about the new value. And it bites hardest where a county has no excess levy, and the land started out worth little. That reading is mine, from the bill's text, not his words.

## p3

He also said there's, quote, "nothing in 2014 that talks about a tax cut." End quote. And he said the money gets, quote, "just transferred to the general fund." End quote.

The general-fund part matches the bill. The fund's net proceeds are certified by the last day of the fiscal year. Then they're moved to the general revenue fund within sixty days of that certification.

But first, the bill counts them as adjusted general revenue collections under section eleven, twenty-one, four-H of the state code. That section is titled, "Future personal income tax reductions."

My read: he's right that the fund doesn't hand anyone a tax cut directly. The fund's name and that cross-reference are the bill's case that it does talk about a tax cut, at one remove. And his account skips that.

## p4

Why does this matter beyond Charleston?

By early September, the state had certified at least four High Impact Data Centers. Penzance, in Berkeley County. A Google-backed complex near Buffalo, in Putnam County. And projects by Nscale and Starwood Digital Ventures, in Mason County.

The Herald-Dispatch reported then that H-B twenty fourteen diverted most of the projects' property tax revenue from local taxing bodies. It said that move was estimated to cost counties and school districts millions.

To be fair to Pack, I don't read him as against the projects. He called data centers, quote, "amazing opportunities." End quote. That was for jobs and investment. And he said there needs to be, quote, "more transparency." End quote. And local input.

He said he'll work with the Legislature to introduce a bill. The session doesn't open until January thirteenth. So for now, this is a promise to watch, not a law.

## p5

One more local note.

Wheeling City Council heard the first reading Tuesday of an ordinance. It regulates data centers in industrial districts. It sets application standards for power demand and water use. The vote is set for December first.

Mayor Denny McGruder said he's been told it's, quote, "the first existing ordinance of this type." End quote. That's in the state. I can't check that claim.
