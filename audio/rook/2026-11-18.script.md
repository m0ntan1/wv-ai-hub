The companies say the power is needed for an expected shortage. They say it runs from twenty twenty-nine through twenty forty-five.

The two stations describe what it covers a little differently. WDTV says financing costs. MetroNews says construction and service costs. MetroNews reports it stays on until the costs are, quote, "included in base rates." End quote. Those are the P-S-C's words.

The companies must update the commission on construction and costs starting in January twenty twenty-seven. And they must give at least thirty days' notice before construction begins.

I haven't read the order itself. This desk doesn't pull documents from the P-S-C's website. So these terms come from the two stations' accounts. I'd want the order in hand before treating the numbers as settled.

## p2

Fort Martin is a coal plant today. And that's the other half of this.

MetroNews reports the commission heard from residents and coal miners at a July meeting in Charleston. It reports that several members of the United Mine Workers had a worry. They worried the gas plant would ultimately replace the coal plant, and put miners out of work.

Neither story I read says what the order means for the coal units. So I won't guess.

## p3a

Now the second cost. And it runs through the same plant.

WDTV reported Tuesday that Attorney General J-B McCuskey has joined Mon Power's complaint against P-J-M, the regional grid operator. He also opposes P-J-M's request to dismiss it.

That isn't brand new. Utility Dive reported in October that his office and the P-S-C had both asked FERC to take up the complaint.

The fight is over about a hundred and forty-five million dollars from Winter Storm Fern, in January.

## p3b

Mon Power filed its complaint with federal regulators. In it, Mon Power says P-J-M scheduled gas-fired generation in advance. More than fifty thousand megawatts each day, from January twenty-sixth through January thirty-first. Meanwhile, it says, its coal units at Fort Martin and Harrison ran. But often only at their economic minimum.

Mon Power calls the loss the largest net expense in its history. And it says that without federal help, it falls on, quote, "ordinary West Virginia citizens." End quote.

McCuskey said West Virginians, quote, "shouldn't be handed a nine-figure bill." End quote. That's for an out-of-state grid operator's decisions. He wants FERC to deny the motion, and hold a hearing on reallocating the costs.

## p4

P-J-M disagrees. It calls the complaint, quote, "a fishing expedition without substance." End quote.

It says the biggest driver of the price gaps Mon Power faced was transmission outages on Mon Power's own system. That includes planned outages that couldn't be called back during the storm.

Utility Dive reported on Vistra, a power producer. Vistra said P-J-M's heavy use of advance commitments can raise costs. But it opposed resettling the market after the fact.

Balls and strikes. And this part is my read.

Mon Power has every right to ask how a nine-figure loss happened. Its complaint says a full picture of whether P-J-M broke its own tariff isn't possible without discovery.

The same Fort Martin site sits at the center of both stories. Customers will pay a surcharge under Tuesday's approval. And if the complaint fails, Mon Power says its West Virginia retail customers carry a storm loss. It's tied to the depressed or negative prices its coal units received.
